1. Special Matrix Types & Algebraic Properties

1.1 Symmetric & Skew-Symmetric Matrices

Let $A \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times n}$.

  • Symmetric Matrix: $A^T = A$ ($a_{ij} = a_{ji}$). All eigenvalues are strictly REAL. Orthogonally diagonalizable ($A = Q \Lambda Q^T$).
  • Skew-Symmetric Matrix: $A^T = -A$ ($a_{ij} = -a_{ji}$).
    • All diagonal elements are identically zero ($a_{ii} = 0$).
    • All eigenvalues are zero or purely imaginary ($\lambda = 0$ or $\pm i\beta$).
    • For odd order $n$, $\det(A) = 0$ (always non-invertible).
WHEN TO USE: Whenever asked about invertibility of $A^T = -A$, check the dimension $n$. If $n$ is odd, $\det(A) = 0$ immediately without any calculation. If given an arbitrary matrix $M$, remember $M + M^T$ is always symmetric, and $M - M^T$ is always skew-symmetric.
GATE TRAP: Skew-symmetric matrices of even order ($2 \times 2, 4 \times 4$) CAN have non-zero determinants! For example, $\begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ -1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$ has determinant $+1$.

1.2 Orthogonal Matrices

$Q^T Q = Q Q^T = I_n \iff Q^{-1} = Q^T$
  • Determinant: $\det(Q) = \pm 1$. (If $\det(Q) = +1$, it represents a pure rotation; if $-1$, rotation + reflection).
  • All eigenvalues have unit modulus: $|\lambda| = 1$ (i.e., $\lambda = \pm 1$ or $e^{i\theta}$).
  • Preserves Euclidean vector length: $\|Q x\|_2 = \|x\|_2$, and preserves inner products: $(Qx)^T (Qy) = x^T y$.
  • The columns (and rows) of $Q$ form an orthonormal basis of $\mathbb{R}^n$.
WHEN TO USE: When asked to invert an orthogonal matrix, simply take the transpose ($Q^{-1} = Q^T$). When asked for the condition number in $L_2$ norm: $\kappa_2(Q) = 1$ (perfectly conditioned).

1.3 Idempotent (Projection) & Nilpotent Matrices

Matrix TypeAlgebraic ConditionEigenvalues ($\lambda$)Key Theorem & Identity

Nilpotent $N^k = 0$ ($k \in \mathbb{N}$) All $\lambda_i = 0$ $\mathbf{\det(N) = 0}$, $\mathbf{\text{Trace}(N) = 0}$; non-zero nilpotent matrix is never diagonalizable.

2. Determinants, Inverses & Adjoints

2.1 The Master Adjoint & Determinant Formulas

For any $n \times n$ matrix $A$:

$A \cdot \text{adj}(A) = \text{adj}(A) \cdot A = \det(A) \cdot I_n$
Operation / QuantityExhaustive FormulaWhen to Use in GATE
Determinant of Adjoint $|\text{adj}(A)| = |A|^{n-1}$ Direct calculation when given order $n$ and $|A|$.
Double Adjoint Determinant $|\text{adj}(\text{adj}(A))| = |A|^{(n-1)^2}$ High-frequency 2-mark question in CS & DA.
Double Adjoint Matrix $\text{adj}(\text{adj}(A)) = |A|^{n-2} A$ When expressing double adjoint in terms of matrix $A$.
Scalar Multiple Determinant $\det(k A) = k^n \det(A)$ Pulling a constant out of an $n \times n$ determinant.
Scalar Multiple of Adjoint $\text{adj}(k A) = k^{n-1} \text{adj}(A)$ Simplifying adjoints with scalar multipliers.
Inverse Formula $A^{-1} = \frac{1}{|A|} \text{adj}(A)$ Calculating inverse when $|A| \neq 0$.
10-SECOND SHORTCUT FOR $2 \times 2$ ADJOINT & INVERSE: For $A = \begin{pmatrix} a & b \\ c & d \end{pmatrix}$: $$\text{adj}(A) = \begin{pmatrix} d & -b \\ -c & a \end{pmatrix} \quad (\text{Swap diagonals, negate off-diagonals})$$
GATE TRAP: Forgetting the power of $n$ in scalar multiplication! $\det(2A) \neq 2\det(A)$. For a $3 \times 3$ matrix, $\det(2A) = 2^3 \det(A) = 8\det(A)$.

2.2 Block Matrix Determinants (Schur Complements)

For block triangular matrices where diagonal blocks $A$ and $B$ are square:

$\det \begin{pmatrix} A & C \\ 0 & B \end{pmatrix} = \det \begin{pmatrix} A & 0 \\ C & B \end{pmatrix} = \det(A) \cdot \det(B)$

For general $2 \times 2$ block matrices where $A$ is invertible:

$\det \begin{pmatrix} A & B \\ C & D \end{pmatrix} = \det(A) \cdot \det(D - C A^{-1} B)$
WHEN TO USE: Used extensively in GATE DA for covariance matrices of partitioned random vectors, and in CS for divide-and-conquer matrix analysis.

3. Matrix Rank, Nullity & Subspaces

3.1 The Rank-Nullity Theorem (Dimension Theorem)

$\text{Rank}(A) + \text{Nullity}(A) = n \quad (\text{Number of Columns / Domain Dimension})$
  • $\text{Rank}(A) = \dim(\text{Col}(A)) = \dim(\text{Row}(A))$.
  • $\text{Nullity}(A) = \dim(\text{Null}(A)) =$ Number of free variables in $Ax = 0$.
  • For $A \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times n}$: $\text{Rank}(A) \le \min(m, n)$.
WHEN TO USE: Whenever given the dimension of the null space or asked for the number of linearly independent solutions to $Ax = 0$.
GATE TRAP: Students often write $\text{Rank} + \text{Nullity} = m$. It is ALWAYS $n$ (the column count / domain dimension), regardless of the number of rows $m$!

3.2 Sylvester's Rank Inequality & Transpose Products

Theorem / IdentityMathematical StatementExam Application
Rank of Sum $\text{Rank}(A + B) \le \text{Rank}(A) + \text{Rank}(B)$ Upper bound on combined matrices.
Rank of Product $\text{Rank}(AB) \le \min(\text{Rank}(A), \text{Rank}(B))$ Product rank cannot exceed factor rank.
Sylvester's Inequality $\text{Rank}(AB) \ge \text{Rank}(A) + \text{Rank}(B) - n$ Lower bound on rank of product ($n$ = inner cols of $A$).
Outer Product Rank $\text{Rank}(u v^T) = 1$ ($u, v \neq 0$) For any $m \times 1$ and $n \times 1$ vectors. Nullity is $n - 1$.
Gram Matrix Rank (DA Core) $\text{Rank}(A^T A) = \text{Rank}(A A^T) = \text{Rank}(A)$ Normal Equations in Least Squares regression.
Gram Matrix Nullspace $\text{Null}(A^T A) = \text{Null}(A)$ Proving $A^T A$ is invertible $\iff A$ has full column rank.

4. Systems of Linear Equations ($Ax = b$)

4.1 The Complete Rouché-Capelli Decision Tree

Form the augmented matrix $[A | b]$ where $A$ is $m \times n$ ($n$ = number of unknowns):

Rank ConditionConsistencyNumber of SolutionsGeometric Meaning
$\text{Rank}(A) < \text{Rank}([A | b])$ Inconsistent NO SOLUTION (0) Planes are parallel or non-intersecting
$\text{Rank}(A) = \text{Rank}([A | b]) = n$ Consistent UNIQUE SOLUTION (1) Planes intersect at a single unique point
$\text{Rank}(A) = \text{Rank}([A | b]) = r < n$ Consistent INFINITELY MANY Intersection along line/subspace ($n - r$ free variables)
WHEN TO USE FOR PARAMETER PROBLEMS (e.g., find $k$ for infinite/no solution):
  1. Write the augmented matrix $[A | b]$.
  2. Apply row operations to reach row-echelon form.
  3. Inspect the bottom row $(0 \quad 0 \quad f(k) \mid g(k))$:
    • No solution: $f(k) = 0$ AND $g(k) \neq 0$ (produces $0 = \text{non-zero}$).
    • Infinitely many solutions: $f(k) = 0$ AND $g(k) = 0$ (entire row vanishes).
    • Unique solution: $f(k) \neq 0$ (pivot exists in every column of $A$).

5. LU Decomposition & Gaussian Elimination

5.1 Doolittle LU Factorization ($A = LU$)

$A = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ l_{21} & 1 & 0 \\ l_{31} & l_{32} & 1 \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} u_{11} & u_{12} & u_{13} \\ 0 & u_{22} & u_{23} \\ 0 & 0 & u_{33} \end{pmatrix}$
  • $L$ is unit lower triangular (1s on the diagonal).
  • $U$ is upper triangular (obtained by forward Gaussian elimination without row scaling).
  • Multiplier Rule: $l_{ij} = \frac{\text{element to eliminate}}{\text{pivot element}}$.
  • Existence Theorem: $A = LU$ without pivoting exists and is unique if and only if **all leading principal minors** are non-singular: $$\det(A_k) \neq 0 \quad \text{for } k = 1, 2, \dots, n-1$$
  • Determinant: $\det(A) = \det(L) \det(U) = (1) \cdot \prod u_{ii} = \prod u_{ii}$.
WHEN TO USE: When asked to find an element $u_{ij}$ in $U$, simply perform elementary row operations $R_i \leftarrow R_i - l_{ik} R_k$ just like standard Gaussian elimination. You do NOT need to multiply out the entire matrix.

6. Eigenvalues, Eigenvectors & Cayley-Hamilton

6.1 The 4 Golden Laws of Eigenvalues

PropertyFormulaFast Inspection Trick
Sum of Eigenvalues $\sum_{i=1}^n \lambda_i = \text{Trace}(A) = \sum a_{ii}$ Instantly eliminates 2 out of 4 options in MCQ!
Product of Eigenvalues $\prod_{i=1}^n \lambda_i = \det(A)$ If matrix is singular ($\det=0$), at least one $\lambda = 0$.
Triangular Matrix $\lambda_i = a_{ii}$ Eigenvalues are directly the diagonal entries!
Powers of Matrix $\text{Eigenvalues of } A^k = \lambda_i^k$ Same eigenvectors $v_i$.
Inverse Matrix $\text{Eigenvalues of } A^{-1} = 1/\lambda_i$ Provided $\lambda_i \neq 0$. Same eigenvectors.
Polynomial Mapping $\text{Eigenvalues of } P(A) = P(\lambda_i)$ If $A$ has $\lambda$, then $A^2 - 3A + 2I$ has $\lambda^2 - 3\lambda + 2$.
15-SECOND SHORTCUT FOR $2 \times 2$ CHARACTERISTIC EQUATION: $$\lambda^2 - \text{Trace}(A) \lambda + \det(A) = 0$$ Never waste time expanding $\det(A - \lambda I)$ element by element for $2 \times 2$!

6.2 Cayley-Hamilton Theorem Applications

Every square matrix satisfies its own characteristic polynomial: $p(A) = 0$.

  • Computing Matrix Inverses: If $\lambda^2 - 4\lambda - 5 = 0$: $$A^2 - 4A - 5I = 0 \implies 5I = A(A - 4I) \implies A^{-1} = \frac{1}{5}(A - 4I)$$
  • Computing High Powers ($A^m$): $$\lambda^m = q(\lambda) p(\lambda) + r(\lambda) \implies A^m = r(A)$$ where $r(\lambda)$ is a polynomial of degree $\le n - 1$.
WHEN TO USE: When asked to express $A^3, A^4,$ or $A^{-1}$ as a linear combination $\alpha A + \beta I$.

6.3 Diagonalization & Multiplicity

  • Algebraic Multiplicity (AM): Multiplicity of $\lambda$ as a root of $\det(A - \lambda I) = 0$.
  • Geometric Multiplicity (GM): $\dim(\text{Null}(A - \lambda I)) = n - \text{Rank}(A - \lambda I)$ (number of independent eigenvectors).
  • Fundamental Inequality: $1 \le GM(\lambda) \le AM(\lambda)$.
  • Diagonalizability Criterion: $A$ is diagonalizable $\iff GM(\lambda) = AM(\lambda)$ for every eigenvalue.
  • Sufficient Condition: If $A$ has $n$ distinct eigenvalues, $A$ is **GUARANTEED** to be diagonalizable.

7. Singular Value Decomposition (SVD) & Quadratic Forms (GATE DA Core)

7.1 Singular Value Decomposition ($A = U \Sigma V^T$)

$A_{m \times n} = U_{m \times m} \Sigma_{m \times n} V^T_{n \times n}$
  • $U$: Orthogonal matrix of **Left Singular Vectors** (eigenvectors of $A A^T$).
  • $V$: Orthogonal matrix of **Right Singular Vectors** (eigenvectors of $A^T A$).
  • $\Sigma$: Diagonal matrix with singular values $\sigma_1 \ge \sigma_2 \ge \dots \ge \sigma_r > 0$.
  • Master Identity:
    $\sigma_i = \sqrt{\lambda_i(A^T A)} = \sqrt{\lambda_i(A A^T)} \ge 0$
  • Singular values are **ALWAYS non-negative real numbers**, even if eigenvalues of $A$ are negative or complex!
Matrix QuantityFormula in Terms of Singular ValuesExam Significance
Rank of $A$ $r = \text{Number of strictly positive } \sigma_i > 0$ Rank equals non-zero singular value count.
Spectral Norm (2-norm) $\|A\|_2 = \sigma_{\max} = \sigma_1$ Maximum magnification factor of vector.
Frobenius Norm $\|A\|_F = \sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^r \sigma_i^2} = \sqrt{\text{Trace}(A^T A)}$ Root-mean-square sum of all elements.
Nuclear Norm $\|A\|_* = \sum_{i=1}^r \sigma_i$ L1-penalty proxy for rank minimization in ML.
Condition Number $\kappa_2(A) = \frac{\sigma_{\max}}{\sigma_{\min}} = \frac{\sigma_1}{\sigma_n}$ Stability of linear systems and gradient descent.
Pseudoinverse $A^+ = V \Sigma^+ U^T$ Minimum-norm Least Squares solution: $\hat{x} = A^+ b$.
WHEN TO USE: When given a diagonal matrix with negative numbers (e.g. $\text{diag}(3, -4)$) and asked for singular values, take absolute values! $\sigma_1 = 4, \sigma_2 = 3$.

7.2 Quadratic Forms & Definiteness Tests

For symmetric matrix $A$: $Q(x) = x^T A x$.

ClassificationEigenvalue ConditionSylvester's Criterion (Leading Principal Minors)
Positive Definite (PD) All $\lambda_i > 0$ $D_1 > 0, D_2 > 0, D_3 > 0, \dots, D_n > 0$
Positive Semi-Definite (PSD) All $\lambda_i \ge 0$ All principal minors (not just leading) $\ge 0$
Negative Definite (ND) All $\lambda_i < 0$ Alternating: $D_1 < 0, D_2 > 0, D_3 < 0, D_4 > 0, \dots$
Indefinite Both positive & negative $\lambda$ Fails both PD and ND tests (saddle point)
GATE TRAP: For $n \ge 3$, having $\det(A) > 0$ and $\text{Trace}(A) > 0$ is NOT sufficient to prove positive definiteness! Counterexample: Eigenvalues $(-1, -1, 4) \implies \det = 4 > 0, \text{Trace} = 2 > 0$, yet indefinite! All leading minors must be tested.
